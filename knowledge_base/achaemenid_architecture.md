# Achaemenid Architecture

## Overview
Achaemenid architecture developed under the Persian Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BCE), 
best known through sites like Persepolis. It combined monumental scale with influences 
from Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Anatolian traditions into a distinct imperial style.

## Defining Features
- Massive stone platforms and terraces as building foundations
- Tall, slender columns with elaborate bull, lion, or griffin-head capitals
- Grand columned audience halls (apadana) capable of holding large ceremonial gatherings
- Relief carvings depicting tribute processions and royal ceremonies along staircases and walls
- Use of both stone and mudbrick, often combined with cedar timber roofing

## Conservation Considerations
- Exposed stone reliefs are highly vulnerable to weathering and erosion, especially in 
  arid, wind-exposed sites
- Column capitals with fine carved detail (animal heads) are prone to fracture at thin, 
  protruding sections
- Mudbrick sections (where used) are far more fragile than stone and require different 
  conservation approaches, including protective coverings
- Many surviving Achaemenid sites are archaeological ruins rather than intact buildings, 
  so conservation often focuses on stabilization rather than restoration
